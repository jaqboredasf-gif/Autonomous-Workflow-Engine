# Competitive positioning

**Status: UNRESEARCHED.** This document defines how the comparison will be made and what would make
it honest. **The cells are empty on purpose** — every one of them is a claim about somebody else's
product, and a claim about a competitor that a judge can falsify is worse than no comparison at all.

`readiness.mjs` scores `differentiation` at band 0 for exactly this reason, and it is one of the
cheapest bands in the whole scorecard to move: an afternoon with four vendor websites and a trial
account.

---

## 1. Compare categories, not invented competitors

Naming a specific company as a competitor invites a judge who knows that company to correct you, and
it is usually wrong anyway — a trades business is not choosing between AWE and a named startup. It
is choosing between AWE and **what it does today**.

The five categories a trades business actually chooses between:

| Category | Concretely, for a business like the first customer |
|---|---|
| **Doing nothing** | Paper, phone calls, a shared spreadsheet, and somebody's memory. **The real incumbent, and the one to take most seriously.** |
| **General-purpose AI assistants** | ChatGPT, Copilot. Used ad hoc by individuals, not by the organization. |
| **Workflow automation / RPA** | Zapier, Power Automate, and the automation features inside existing tools. |
| **Construction / ERP software** | Procore, Buildertrend, QuickBooks purchase orders, and the procurement modules of general-contractor suites. |
| **Custom software or a consultant** | Somebody builds you a thing. Common in this industry and the closest substitute for AWE. |

Candidates are drawn from what the first customer actually uses today — `docs/planning/CURRENT_WORKFLOW.md`
§5 — rather than from a market map.

## 2. The dimensions worth comparing

Only dimensions where a real difference exists and can be shown. Anything else is padding that
invites scrutiny for no gain.

| Dimension | The question |
|---|---|
| **Execution** | Does it *do* the work, or tell somebody what to do? |
| **Organizational context** | Does it know this company's rules, roles and approval limits — or must a person supply them each time? |
| **Governance** | Are authority limits enforced by the system, or by trusting the user? |
| **Auditability** | Can you reconstruct why it did what it did, afterwards? |
| **Measurable outcome** | Does it report whether the *objective* was achieved, or only that a task completed? |
| **Cross-workflow reuse** | Does a second business process reuse the same machinery, or is it a second project? |
| **Deployment model** | What does it cost, in time and engineering, to put it in a second company? |

**The sentence to aim for** — testable, and rare in the category:

> Most workflow tools report that a task completed. AWE reports whether the organization's objective
> was achieved, which is a different question and usually a lower number — and it can show you the
> difference.

**Do not use that sentence in a pitch until the table below is filled in and the claim has survived
it.** It is currently a hypothesis about other people's products.

## 3. The comparison table

Left empty deliberately. Filling it in is one afternoon of real research and is one of the highest
value-per-hour tasks available.

### Rules for filling it in

1. **Cite the source for every cell**, with a retrieval date. A vendor's own documentation is
   acceptable; a competitor comparison page written by one of their rivals is not.
2. **No red-X / green-tick chart.** Every one of these categories does something well, and a chart
   where the author's column is the only one with ticks is read — correctly — as marketing. Use
   words. "Reports task completion" is more convincing than a cross, because it is checkable.
3. **Name what the alternatives do better.** RPA is faster to set up. ERP already has the data. A
   consultant will build exactly what you asked for. Saying so buys the credibility that makes the
   remaining claims land.
4. **Where AWE loses, say where.** AWE has one production deployment and no integrations. Procore
   has thousands of customers. That comparison is not survivable on maturity and should not be
   attempted; the comparison is on *what the software does*, not on how established it is.
5. **Prefer a dimension we can demonstrate on stage** over one we can only assert. The
   objective-versus-completion distinction is demonstrable today and almost nothing in the category
   does it.

### The honest cell about AWE, written first

> **AWE:** one production deployment, one capability with production evidence, no integrations with
> existing accounting or construction software, and no customers outside the first company. What it
> does that the others do not is execute governed work end to end and then report whether the
> organization's objective was achieved — separately from whether the task completed.

If a comparison cannot be made without that paragraph next to it, the comparison is not ready.

## 4. Where this appears in the presentation

- **Beat 11 (defensibility) — Q&A only.** Volunteered, a competitor slide sounds defensive.
  Requested, it is decisive.
- **Executive summary — Differentiation section.** Written, where a reader can check it.
- **Never in the video.** Sixty seconds cannot hold a comparison, and a comparison is not what gets
  a video shared.

Two of the eight judging criteria — **uniqueness** and **feasibility** — depend on this being real.
It is currently the cheapest unserved criterion in the whole pitch.
