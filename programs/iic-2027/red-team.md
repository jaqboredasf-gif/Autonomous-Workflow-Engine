# How this loses

Fifteen ways. Each is a real failure mode, each has a mitigation, and none is hidden. A risk
register that flatters the project is a second copy of the pitch.

**Live today** are risks 1, 3 and 13. Everything else is conditional on choices not yet made.

---

### 1. "The audience doesn't understand what it does." — **LIVE, and the worst one**

The most likely way this loses, and the one the engineering makes *more* likely rather than less.
Two of eight criteria are articulation and overall impression.

**Mitigation.** The one sentence is canonical, banned vocabulary is enforced by the suite, and beat 4
is `NOT_READY` until three non-technical people restate it correctly an hour later. That test has
not been run, which is why it is the weakest beat in the pitch.
**Residual risk:** high, and cheap to reduce — an afternoon.

### 2. "AWE is too broad."

"A platform for autonomous business execution" is either enormous or meaningless, and a judge has
four minutes to decide which.

**Mitigation.** The pitch is about one company, one workflow, one result. The breadth is *revealed*
in beat 8 after the specific thing has been shown, never claimed at the start.
**Residual risk:** medium — depends entirely on discipline in delivery.

### 3. "This looks like custom software for one company." — **LIVE**

It is a fair reading of the evidence today: one deployment, one customer, and a synthetic second
company that must be described as synthetic.

**Mitigation.** Beat 8, the second capability, the measured configuration percentage, and the
provisioning of a second organization with no source change. **The only real fix is a real second
organization**, which is why `external_want` sits high in the planner.
**Residual risk:** high until an external deployment exists.

### 4. "The problem doesn't seem painful."

Purchasing paperwork is not visibly dramatic, and judges who have never worked in a trades business
may file it as an inconvenience.

**Mitigation.** One specific incident with one person in it, in beat 1. Not a category, not a
statistic. The measured baseline, once it exists, converts an inconvenience into a cost.
**Residual risk:** medium, falling sharply once the baseline is measured.

### 5. "Hours returned are tiny."

A plausible outcome. If a purchase request took eighteen minutes and now takes four, the annual
figure for one small company may be unimpressive on a slide.

**Mitigation.** Do not lead with the absolute figure. Lead with *proportion* and with what the
architecture does with it — the same measurement applies to every workflow, and purchasing is one.
**Never inflate it**, and never extrapolate to "if every contractor in America…", which is the
single most discrediting move available.
**Residual risk:** unknown until measured. Prepare for the number being small.

### 6. "There's no outside market." — **LIVE in substance**

Zero external interviews today. Beat 7 is `NOT_READY` and cannot be filled by anything but
conversations.

**Mitigation.** None available in software. The planner has recommended customer discovery from the
day it was written.
**Residual risk:** high, entirely under the founder's control, cheapest fix in the whole project.

### 7. "There's no revenue."

True, and there may still be none in April.

**Mitigation.** **There is no revenue criterion in the published rubric.** Answer honestly and
briefly, then move to what *is* scored: feasibility, cost and ease of implementation. A long defence
of the absence of revenue converts a non-scored gap into the memorable moment of the pitch.
**Residual risk:** low, if the answer is short.

### 8. "The demo is too technical."

Real risk when the founder built the thing.

**Mitigation.** Narrate the *human*, not the software: "this used to be a phone call to the office."
60–75 seconds, one workflow, no configuration screens, no prompts being typed.
**Residual risk:** medium — this is a rehearsal problem, and rehearsal fixes it.

### 9. "The architecture overwhelms the business."

The strongest engineering in this project is invisible and hard to resist explaining.

**Mitigation.** The capability-reveal policy: kernel, runtime and AXIS are Q&A-only. No acronym that
is not a customer's in the spoken pitch.
**Residual risk:** medium. The temptation is strongest in Q&A, where a technical question feels like
permission.

### 10. "The expansion vision feels delusional."

A student claiming to be building a platform for autonomous business execution, with one customer.

**Mitigation.** The vision beat has a required evidence slot and a kill condition: **cut it entirely
if the second capability is not visible.** Two sentences maximum. It is delivered as a direction,
not a projection.
**Residual risk:** medium — and self-limiting, because the kill condition removes the beat rather
than softening it.

### 11. "The second capability feels unrelated."

Purchasing and a second workflow may look like two projects rather than one platform.

**Mitigation.** Show the *seam*, not the second workflow: the same measurement boundary, the same
governance, the same provisioning. The claim is "this machinery is reused", and that is the visible
thing.
**Residual risk:** medium.

### 12. "The founder story feels inflated."

The one part of the pitch a judge cannot verify — which makes any exaggeration in it fatal if found.

**Mitigation.** `founder-story.md` lists what the repository can attest and what must be confirmed
by the founder before it is said. Nothing is invented, and the relationship to the business is
stated plainly rather than left vague.
**Residual risk:** low if the discipline holds.

### 13. "The pitch is 80% future and 20% reality." — **LIVE**

Today it is worse than that. Five of ten claims in the stack are unproven.

**Mitigation.** The evidence slots make this visible rather than avoidable, and the beat kill
conditions mean weak beats are *cut* rather than padded. A four-minute pitch built only from what is
real is better than a six-minute pitch that is mostly forecast — and the format enforces four.
**Residual risk:** high today, and it is the whole reason the evidence deadline is January.

### 14. "The judges remember PCC but not AWE."

The failure where the demo works too well: the audience remembers a purchasing tool.

**Mitigation.** The wow moment exists for exactly this: *"purchasing is not what AWE is; it is the
first thing we pointed it at."* AWE is named at the start and the end; PCC is never named aloud.
**Residual risk:** medium — measurable in mock pitches by asking the listener afterwards what the
company does.

### 15. "The judges remember AI but not the customer problem."

The other direction: it becomes "the AI project" among the water bottles and the rehab glove.

**Mitigation.** The word "AI" appears once, in the contrast line, and the pitch opens on a business
rather than on a technology. The close returns to people, not to software.
**Residual risk:** medium.

---

## The three tests that would find the rest

1. **Ask a mock-pitch listener, an hour later, what the company does.** If the answer is "AI for
   construction" or "a purchasing app", risks 14 or 15 are live and the fix is the opening.
2. **Have somebody attack the numbers.** Every figure should walk back to executions in one
   command. Any figure that cannot must come out of the deck.
3. **Deliver the three admissions — hours, external users, revenue — to somebody unfriendly.** If
   they land badly, the problem is delivery, not evidence, and it is fixed by repetition.
